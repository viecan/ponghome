Pips Explained: How to Play Against Long Pips and Short Pips

Pips rubber breaks all the normal spin rules — here's what long and short pips actually do to the ball, and how to adjust your game against each one.

Pong Home Team · Equipment · 7 min read
PH
Pong Home Team · Coach-written equipment guide

Most table tennis rubber is "inverted" — a smooth topsheet over a layer of sponge, which is what the vast majority of players use and what most spin-reading intuition is built around. Pips rubber flips that setup: instead of a smooth surface, small rubber nubs (pips) face outward, either with or without a sponge layer underneath. The result is ball behavior that doesn't follow the spin rules most players have internalized, which is exactly why playing against pips — especially for the first time — can feel confusing and frustrating, even for otherwise strong players.

What Pips Actually Are

Pips rubber comes in a range of lengths, generally grouped into three categories:

Pips typeGeneral characteristics
Short pipsLow spin generation, fast and flat contact, popular for close-to-table blocking and counter-attacking styles
Medium pipsA middle ground between short and long — some spin capability with some of the disruptive, unpredictable effects of longer pips
Long pipsVery low spin generation of their own, but strong at reversing or neutralizing an opponent's incoming spin, often used defensively

Some pips rubbers also have no sponge layer underneath at all ("no-sponge" pips), which further reduces speed and power while emphasizing the spin-reversal effect, especially common on long pips used defensively close to the table.

Why Pips Feel So Different to Play Against

With standard inverted rubber, spin behaves in a fairly intuitive way: heavy topspin comes back as topspin (perhaps altered by the returning player's racket angle), heavy backspin comes back as backspin, and so on — the physics stay consistent, and experienced players build their return technique around that consistency. Pips, particularly long pips, disrupt that consistency because the nubs interact with the ball completely differently than a smooth surface does. Depending on the specific rubber and the contact used, long pips can reverse incoming spin, dampen it dramatically, or produce unpredictable, "floaty" returns that don't behave like a normal shot at all.

This is why pips are often described as a "spin disruptor" rather than a spin generator in their own right — a player using long pips isn't usually trying to overpower opponents with their own spin, but instead using the rubber's unusual return characteristics to induce mistakes from opponents who are used to reading and reacting to normal inverted rubber.

Long Pips

How Do You Play Against Long Pips?

Don't feed heavy spin, shorten your strokes, and watch the racket angle rather than trusting normal spin physics.

The single most important adjustment against long pips is to stop assuming spin will behave the way it normally does. Because long pips tend to reverse or flatten incoming spin, a heavy topspin shot you send at a long pips opponent can come back with backspin-like characteristics, and vice versa — which means the return often doesn't match what your shot "should" produce against inverted rubber.

  • Avoid feeding heavy spin into long pips when possible. The heavier the spin you put in, the more unpredictable and potentially reversed the return tends to be — flatter, more controlled shots often produce more predictable results.
  • Use shorter, more controlled strokes on your return rather than a full swing, since the incoming ball's spin character is harder to read at a glance.
  • Watch the opponent's racket angle at contact more than you'd normally need to, since it's a more reliable cue than trying to read spin off ball flight alone against pips.
  • Vary your placement and pace rather than relying on spin to win points, since spin is the exact tool long pips are designed to neutralize.

Many players' first instinct against long pips is to push even harder with spin, assuming more spin means more advantage — this is usually the wrong instinct, since it plays directly into what long pips rubber is built to disrupt.

Short Pips

How Do You Play Against Short Pips?

Expect faster, flatter returns with less spin, and be ready for quick counter-attacks close to the table.

Short pips behave differently from long pips in an important way: rather than emphasizing spin disruption, short pips are generally built for speed, flat contact, and close-to-table blocking or counter-attacking. A short pips opponent typically isn't trying to confuse your spin reading as much as a long pips player would — they're trying to take time away from you with quick, flat, hard-to-read shots.

  • Expect your own spin serves to "bite" less once returned, since short pips absorb and transmit less spin than inverted rubber — a heavy serve that would normally draw a weak return can come back flatter and faster than expected.
  • Be ready for quick blocks and counters rather than long spin-heavy rallies, since short pips players often thrive at close range with fast exchanges.
  • Keep your own shots controlled rather than overhitting — since short pips returns tend to come back with less spin to work with, overcommitting on power can send routine shots off the end of the table.
  • Attack with placement and pace changes instead of relying purely on spin, since spin alone is less effective at unsettling a short pips player than it would be against inverted rubber.

The Bigger Picture: Why Facing Pips Trips People Up

The core reason pips opponents feel harder than their overall skill level might suggest is pattern mismatch — most players' return technique is built almost entirely around reading and reacting to inverted rubber, simply because it's what the majority of players and training partners use. The first few times facing pips, that pattern-matching instinct actively works against you, since the cues you're used to relying on don't map the same way. This is a normal adjustment period, not a sign you're playing poorly, and it tends to improve quickly with repeated exposure.
