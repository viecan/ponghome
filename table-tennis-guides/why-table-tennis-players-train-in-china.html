Why More Table Tennis Players Are Traveling to Train in China

Club players from Canada, the US, and Europe are booking flights to train in the country that dominates the sport. Here is what the camps are actually like, what they cost, and whether the trip is worth it.

Pong Home Insights Hub · Training · 10 min read
PH
Pong Home Insights Hub · Coach-written training guide

Ten years ago, a trip to China to train table tennis was something only national team hopefuls did. Today it is a growing part of the amateur calendar. Club players in their thirties, forties, and fifties are spending vacation time in Shanghai, Beijing, or Shandong province doing multiball drills for six hours a day, then coming home and telling everyone at the club about it. This guide explains why the trend is growing, what a Chinese training camp actually involves, what it costs, and who it really makes sense for.

Why China, and Why Now

The simple answer is that China has dominated international table tennis for decades, and the system that produced that dominance is now partially open to outsiders. Provincial training centers, private academies, and university sports programs have realized there is demand from foreign players who want a taste of the real thing, and many of them now run structured camps aimed at exactly that audience.

Three things changed at once. Flights became more affordable and visa-free or simplified entry expanded for many countries. Chinese coaches who once worked only within the national system moved into private coaching, where foreign clients pay well. And social media made it obvious to club players everywhere what a Chinese practice hall looks like: rows of tables, buckets of balls, and coaches who correct your elbow position without slowing the feed. Once players saw it, a lot of them wanted it.

What Training in China Actually Looks Like

The Daily Schedule

A typical camp day runs two sessions. Morning is usually 9:00 to 12:00, afternoon is 2:30 to 5:30, with a lunch and rest block in between that you will need more than you expect. Some camps add a lighter evening session for match play or physical conditioning. That is five to six hours of table time a day, most days of the week, and it is the single biggest difference from club training back home where two hours twice a week is considered serious.

Multiball Is the Core

Chinese coaching is built around multiball. The coach stands at one end with a bucket of balls and feeds you a set pattern: forehand, forehand, backhand, pivot, over and over, for several minutes without a break. Then a new pattern. Then another. You hit hundreds of balls in the time a normal rally practice would give you dozens. The purpose is repetition under fatigue, so the stroke becomes automatic even when your legs are tired and your focus is slipping.

Corrections Are Direct and Physical

Do not expect long explanations. A Chinese coach will stop the feed, walk over, physically move your racket arm or shift your stance, say a few words, and start feeding again. Feedback is constant and specific. Many foreign players describe this as the most valuable part of the trip, because they discover technical flaws that were never pointed out in years of club play.

Sparring Partners at a Different Level

The sparring partners at a good camp are often former provincial team players, current college players, or teenagers on the academy track. Playing against them daily raises your baseline for what a "normal" ball looks like. When you return home, club-level topspin feels slower, and that recalibration alone can be worth a few rating points.

Tip: Bring at least two racket setups and a spare sheet of your main rubber. Six hours a day wears rubber fast, and your usual brands may not be stocked locally. Check current sheet prices on the Discount Center before you leave.

The Main Types of Camps

  • Private academies for foreign players. The most common option. English-speaking coordinators, accommodation arranged, coaches assigned per player or per small group. Easiest to book, highest cost per day.
  • Provincial or city training centers. You train alongside domestic athletes on a real development pathway. Less hand-holding, more authentic, harder to arrange without a contact.
  • University sports programs. Some universities with strong table tennis programs run short summer or winter sessions. Good value, often tied to a fixed calendar.
  • Group tours organized from abroad. A coach or club from your home country arranges the trip for a group. Social, simple, and usually a little pricier because of the organizer margin.

What It Costs

Prices move with the city and the amount of one-on-one coaching included, but a realistic range for a camp that caters to foreign players is roughly 100 to 200 USD per day covering training, a shared or private room, and meals. Smaller cities and provincial centers can be cheaper. Big-name coaches or fully private coaching all day can push well beyond that.

For a two week trip, budget roughly:

  • Training, room, and meals: 1,400 to 2,800 USD
  • Round trip flight from North America or Europe: 900 to 1,600 USD depending on season
  • Visa, insurance, local transport, extras: 200 to 400 USD

All in, most players land somewhere between 2,500 and 4,500 USD for two weeks. Compared with the cost of one-on-one coaching at home, where a single hour can run 60 to 120 USD, two weeks of full-day coached training in China is often cheaper per hour on the table, and that is a big part of why the trend keeps growing.

The Honest Downsides

The Volume Can Break You Before It Builds You

Six hours a day is brutal if you arrive out of shape. Knee, shoulder, and lower back complaints are common in the first week, and some players lose training days to injury. Two months of conditioning and footwork work before the trip is the difference between a great camp and a painful one. Our footwork guide is a good place to start.

Technique Changes Take Longer Than Two Weeks

A coach may rebuild your forehand in China, but a rebuilt stroke needs months of reinforcement to become permanent. Players who come home and go straight back to unstructured club play often revert within a couple of months and wonder where the improvement went.

Coaching Style Is Not for Everyone

The approach is demanding, repetitive, and low on discussion. If you are the kind of player who wants to understand the "why" behind every adjustment, you may find it frustrating. Many players love it precisely because it removes overthinking. Know which type you are before you book.

Quality Varies

Not every camp with a glossy website has strong coaches. Ask for the coaching staff's playing and coaching background, ask how many players share one coach, and look for reviews from players at a similar level to yours, not just from beginners who were impressed by anything.

Keep in mind: the players who get the most out of China are not the ones who train hardest during the trip. They are the ones who arrive with two or three specific goals, write down every correction, and keep drilling those corrections for the three months after they come home.

Who Should Go, and Who Should Wait

Go if: you are an intermediate or advanced club player with stable strokes, you can physically handle high volume, you have a clear weakness you want rebuilt, and you have a coach or a structured plan waiting for you back home.

Wait if: you are still learning basic strokes, you have not trained more than a few hours a week recently, or you are hoping the trip alone will jump your rating. Local coaching first, China second, is usually the better order. If you want to get the fundamentals in place before you go, one-on-one coaching is the fastest route and you can find out more on the Pong Home homepage.

How to Prepare If You Decide to Go

  • Build your fitness for eight weeks. Footwork ladders, shadow play, and leg conditioning matter more than extra table time.
  • Pick two or three goals. A specific stroke, a specific footwork pattern, a specific serve. Tell your coach on day one.
  • Record everything. Video your sessions, especially the moments a coach corrects you. It is your reference for the next six months.
  • Sort your equipment before you leave. Check the Gear Index to make sure your rubber and blade setup suits the higher pace you are about to face, and pack spares.
  • Plan the follow through. Book a few coaching sessions at home for the weeks after you return so the changes stick.

Common Questions About Training in China

How much does a table tennis training camp in China cost?

Most camps that cater to foreign players charge roughly 100 to 200 USD per day for training, accommodation, and meals, so a two week trip usually lands between 2,000 and 4,000 USD once flights and a visa are added. Prices vary by city, by how much one-on-one coaching is included, and by whether you book directly or through an agency.

Do I need to speak Chinese to train in China?

No. Camps that accept foreign players usually have at least one English speaking coordinator, and table tennis coaching itself relies heavily on demonstration, feeding, and hand signals. A few basic Mandarin phrases help with daily life and build goodwill, but they are not required to get value from the training.

What level do you need to be to train in China?

Most camps accept anyone from committed beginners upward, but the trip pays off most for intermediate and advanced club players who already have stable strokes and can handle five to six hours of table time per day. Complete beginners often get more from local coaching first.

Is training in China worth it for a club player?

It is worth it if you go with specific goals, arrive physically prepared, and have a plan to keep training when you return. The biggest gains are in stroke consistency, footwork, and mindset rather than instant rating jumps. Without a follow through plan at home, most of the improvement fades within a few months.

The Bottom Line

Training in China is not a shortcut, and it is not magic. It is a concentrated dose of the two things most club players never get enough of: volume and correction. For a prepared player with clear goals and a plan for afterward, it can compress a year of progress into a few weeks and permanently change what "hard training" means to you. For an unprepared player, it is an expensive and exhausting holiday. The difference is decided before you board the plane.

Want to be ready before you book the flight?

One-on-one coaching builds the stable strokes and footwork base that make a high-volume camp pay off, and gives you a plan to keep the gains when you come home.
