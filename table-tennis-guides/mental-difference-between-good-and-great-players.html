Mental Game

The Mental Difference Between Good Players and Great Players

At the top of any club, the strokes look similar. What separates the player who wins the close ones from the player who almost does is happening between the ears. Here is what that difference actually is, and how to train it.

Pong Home Insights Hub · Mental Game · 9 min read
PH
Pong Home Insights Hub · Coach-written mental game piece

Every club has a player who is clearly good. Clean strokes, solid footwork, wins most of the time, and then loses 11-9 in the fifth to someone with worse technique and a calmer face. Every club also has a player who seems to win exactly those matches. Watch closely and the difference is rarely the forehand. It is what each player does after a missed serve at 8-8, how they choose a shot when their legs are tired, and what they are thinking about between points. This article breaks down those differences, one by one, and shows you how to practice each of them.

Good Players Play the Score. Great Players Play the Point.

A good player at 9-9 is thinking about 9-9. The number leaks into the stroke: a shorter swing, a safer serve, a push where a loop was the right call. A great player at 9-9 is thinking about the next ball. Where is the serve going, what is the likely return, what is the plan if it comes to the backhand. The score is information, not a mood.

This is not a trick of positive thinking. It is a habit of attention. Great players have practiced pointing their focus at a specific, controllable task so many times that it becomes the default under pressure. Good players still have to fight for it, and in a close game the fight is usually lost.

Good Players React to Mistakes. Great Players Reset.

Everyone misses. The difference is what happens in the four seconds afterward. A good player replays the error, tightens up, and often makes a second mistake caused entirely by the first. A great player has a reset routine: a breath, a glance at the racket, a step back, a fixed phrase in their head, and the point is over. The next point starts clean.

You can see this from across the hall. Great players look the same after a net cord winner and after an unforced error into the net. That neutrality is not because they do not care. It is because they have learned that emotion spent on the last point is taken directly out of the next one.

Tip: Build a physical reset that takes under five seconds and do it after every single point in practice, won or lost. If you only use it in matches, it will not be automatic when you need it. Our guide to pre-match routines covers how to build the rest of the ritual around it.

Good Players Have a Game. Great Players Have a Plan.

A good player brings their style to every match and hopes it works. A great player brings their style plus a specific plan for this opponent: which serve to open with, which side to attack under pressure, what to do when the plan is not working. The plan can be simple, three or four lines is enough, but it exists before the first point, and it is adjusted between games.

The mental payoff is that decisions get made in advance, when the mind is calm, rather than in real time, when it is not. At 9-9 the great player already knows what serve they are going to use. The good player is choosing, and choosing under pressure is where safe, predictable, losing choices come from. If you want to build this skill, start with finding your opponent's weakness.

Good Players Want to Win. Great Players Are Willing to Lose the Right Way.

This one sounds backwards, so it deserves care. A great player would rather lose a match playing the right shot at the right moment than win it by pushing every ball and hoping the opponent misses. Not because winning does not matter, but because they are playing a longer game. A player who is willing to loop the third ball at 10-10, miss it, and loop it again next week is the player who eventually stops missing it. A player who protects every close point with a safe push never builds the shot that wins close points at the next level.

Good players are often trapped by their own results. They win enough with a cautious game that they never feel forced to change it, and so they stay good. It connects directly to the plateau problem most long-term players hit.

Good Players Blame. Great Players Own.

After a loss, listen to the explanation. Good players talk about the net cords, the lighting, the opponent's strange rubber, the warm-up they did not get. Great players talk about the two serves they should have varied and the backhand that kept going long. The great player is not being noble. They are being practical, because a loss you own gives you something to work on and a loss you blame gives you nothing.

This shows up in practice too. Great players seek out feedback, watch their own video, and ask stronger players direct questions. Good players tend to protect the story they already believe about their own game.

Good Players Feel Pressure. Great Players Feel Pressure and Play Anyway.

A common myth is that great players do not get nervous. They do. Their hands sweat at 9-9 like everyone else's. The difference is that they have separated the feeling from the decision. Nerves are allowed to exist; nerves are not allowed to pick the shot. That separation is built by repeatedly playing pressured points in training until the sensation is familiar enough to ignore.

How to Train the Difference

None of this is a personality trait you either have or do not. Each of these habits can be drilled the same way a stroke can. Here is how.

  • Start practice games at 8-8. Play best of seven games that each begin at 8-8. You get twenty pressured endings in the time a normal match gives you two.
  • Attach a cost to one specific error. For example, any push that pops up long loses the game outright. It forces the aggressive decision you avoid in matches.
  • Practice the reset after every point. Breath, racket, phrase, step. In drills, in games, every time, until you do not notice you are doing it.
  • Write a three-line plan before every match. Opening serve, pressure serve, and what to change if losing. Review it between games.
  • Keep a match journal. Three lines after every match: what worked, what failed, one thing to drill. Read it before the next session.
  • Play stronger opponents and lose on purpose. Choose the right shot, not the safe one, and let the result be whatever it is. The shot is the goal for that session, not the score.
Keep in mind: mental skills fade faster than strokes. A reset routine you have not used for a month will not be there at 9-9. Treat these habits like any other part of your training plan, with regular reps, not something you switch on for a tournament.

Common Questions About the Mental Game

Is the mental game really more important than technique in table tennis?

Not more important, but it decides matches once technique is roughly equal. Below a certain level, technique wins. Between two players of similar ability, the one who manages pressure, keeps a clear head after mistakes, and makes better decisions under fatigue wins far more often.

How do great table tennis players stay calm at 9-9?

They rely on routine, not willpower. A fixed between-point ritual, a simple tactical plan they have already decided on, and a habit of focusing on the next serve rather than the score keep their attention on process. Calm is a trained output, not a personality trait.

Can you train mental toughness for table tennis?

Yes. Practice matches that start at 8-8, playing points where a specific error costs you the game, keeping a match journal, and drilling a reset routine after mistakes all build mental habits the same way multiball builds strokes. It needs to be practiced deliberately, not hoped for on match day.

The Bottom Line

Good and great players often have the same strokes. What they do not share is where their attention goes under pressure, what they do in the seconds after a mistake, whether decisions are made before the match or during it, and how they explain a loss to themselves afterward. Every one of those is a habit, and every habit can be trained. The gap between good and great is smaller than it looks from the sideline. It is also more deliberate than most players are willing to make it.
