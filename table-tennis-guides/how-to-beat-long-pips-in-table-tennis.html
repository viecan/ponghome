How to Beat Long Pips in Table Tennis

Long pips quietly breaks the spin rules you've built your game around. Here's what to change about your serve, your rallies, and your mindset to actually beat it.

Pong Home Team · Technique · 7 min read
PH
Pong Home Team · Coach-written technique guide

Long pips are one of the most disruptive styles in table tennis, and for good reason: the rubber quietly breaks the spin rules most players have built their entire game around. If you keep losing to a long pips opponent despite feeling like the better all-round player, the problem usually isn't your technique in general. It's a handful of specific habits that play directly into what long pips is designed to exploit. Here's how to fix that.

Understand What Long Pips Actually Does First

Long pips rubber has small nubs facing outward instead of a smooth topsheet, which changes how it grips and releases the ball compared to standard inverted rubber. Rather than absorbing and returning spin the way inverted rubber does, long pips tends to dampen, flatten, or in some cases reverse the spin you send at it. That means a heavy topspin shot can come back looking more like backspin, and a heavy backspin push can come back flatter and faster than expected. The rubber is a spin disruptor more than a spin generator, which is exactly why it feels so unfamiliar the first few times you face it.

Stop Feeding It Heavy Spin

The single most common mistake against long pips is doubling down on spin, assuming that more spin equals more advantage the way it would against a normal opponent. Against long pips, heavier spin usually just produces a more unpredictable return, since the nubs are what's disrupting your spin rather than a smooth surface that would normally cooperate with it.

  • Use flatter, more controlled shots rather than your heaviest loop or serve, especially early in a rally.
  • Vary your spin intentionally rather than relying on one heavy shot repeatedly, since a predictable pattern is easier for a long pips player to read and counter.
  • Watch the racket, not the ball, since long pips returns often don't behave the way ball flight alone would suggest.

Attack the Short Ball, Not the Spin

Long pips rubber generally can't generate much attacking power of its own, which means a short or high ball from a long pips opponent is usually a genuine opportunity rather than a trap. Many players hesitate to attack against long pips out of habit, worried the return will come back strangely, but a clearly short or high ball should be punished the same way it would against any other style.

Tip: Practice recognizing the difference between a controlled long pips return (low, tight to the net, hard to attack safely) and a defensive mistake (short or popped up). Only the second one is worth attacking aggressively.

Serve With Placement and Variety, Not Just Power

Since long pips disrupts spin more than it struggles with placement or pace, your serve strategy should lean on variety and location rather than trying to overpower it with one heavy spin serve. Mix short and long serves, change your toss and motion between points, and avoid settling into one predictable serving pattern.

Play the Long Game

Rallies against long pips are often longer and less explosive than rallies against inverted rubber opponents, and that's usually by design on their part. Going in expecting a longer, more patient rally, rather than looking for one big shot to end the point, tends to produce far better results than trying to force an early winner.

Putting It All Together

Beating long pips is less about technical firepower and more about recalibrating your expectations for how spin behaves. Once you stop feeding heavy spin, start watching the racket for cues instead of the ball, and start treating short or high balls as real opportunities rather than traps, long pips opponents become far more manageable.
