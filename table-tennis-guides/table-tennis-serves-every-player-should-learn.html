How to Serve in Table Tennis: 10 Serves Every Player Should Learn

Serving is the one shot in table tennis you fully control. Here are 10 serves worth adding to your game, from the fundamental pendulum serve to disguised no-spin serves that confuse even experienced opponents.

Pong Home Team · Technique · 9 min read
PH
Pong Home Team · Coach-written technique guide

Of all the shots in table tennis, the serve is the only one where you have complete control, no incoming ball to react to, no opponent's spin to read, just your own toss and your own racket motion. That's exactly why it's worth investing real practice time here: a strong, varied serve arsenal can win points outright, or at minimum put your opponent on the back foot before the rally even starts. Below are 10 serves that show up constantly from club level through competitive play, what each one does, and when to reach for it.

1
Forehand

Pendulum Serve

The most widely used serve in the modern game. With the racket's forehand side facing the ball, swing in a side-to-side "pendulum" motion, brushing across and slightly down through contact using mostly wrist and forearm. Small changes in contact point let you disguise heavy sidespin, light spin, or a near-no-spin variation from what looks like the same motion.

Use when: You want a reliable, all-purpose serve that's hard to read consistently.
2
Backhand

Reverse Pendulum Serve

The mirror image of the pendulum, hit from the backhand side. The racket swings the opposite direction, brushing across the ball to produce sidespin that curves the other way. Many players find it easier to disguise contact point on this serve since the motion feels less natural to read for opponents used to seeing forehand pendulum serves.

Use when: Mixing up spin direction against an opponent who's adjusted to your forehand pendulum.
3
Forehand

Tomahawk Serve

Named for the chopping, axe-like motion of the racket. Contact is made with the backhand side of the racket while standing in a forehand-ready position, brushing outward and down to create a mix of sidespin and backspin that curves away from a right-handed opponent's forehand. It's visually distinct from a pendulum serve, which makes it a useful change-up.

Use when: You want a serve with a completely different toss motion to reset your opponent's read.
4
Backhand

Backhand Serve

A straightforward serve struck from the backhand side with the racket facing forward, typically producing backspin or a light sidespin-backspin mix. It's simpler mechanically than the reverse pendulum, making it a good starting point for players still building their serve variety, while still being effective at faster paces.

Use when: You need a quick, low-risk serve, especially useful under time pressure or at the start of a match.
5
Forehand

Punch Serve

A short, fast, low-spin serve hit with a compact forward "punching" motion rather than a brushing one. Because there's minimal spin, the ball travels flat and fast, aiming to rush the opponent before they can set up a strong attacking return.

Use when: You want speed and surprise over spin, good against opponents who read spin well but are slower to react to pace.
6
Forehand

Hook Serve

A sidespin-heavy serve where the ball curves noticeably in the air before landing, produced by brushing sharply around the side of the ball rather than underneath or across it. The pronounced curve can make the ball's landing spot harder to judge even for players who read the spin correctly.

Use when: You want to exploit an opponent's positioning by curving the ball wide of where they expect it to land.
7
Advanced

High-Toss Serve

A variation on any of the above serves, but with a toss well beyond the legal 16 cm minimum, often a meter or more. The extra height and hang time let gravity add extra downward acceleration into contact, allowing for noticeably heavier spin than a standard-height toss on the same motion.

Use when: You want to add a spin-heavy weapon to your arsenal, or disrupt an opponent's timing with the visual difference of a much higher toss.
8
Placement

Short Backspin Serve

A low-power serve aimed to bounce twice on the opponent's side of the table if left untouched, landing close to the net with heavy backspin. Because it's short, the opponent can't generate much power on their return without risking the ball going into the net, which limits their attacking options.

Use when: You want to prevent an aggressive attacking return and control the pace of the rally from the first shot.
9
Placement

Half-Long Serve

A serve placed deliberately just past the theoretical "second bounce" point, long enough that it's tempting to attack, but tricky to time cleanly because of how close it lands to that boundary. It's often paired with heavy or deceptive spin to punish opponents who commit to an aggressive swing too early.

Use when: You want to bait an early, off-balance attack from an opponent who over-commits to short balls.
10
Deception

No-Spin ("Ghost") Serve

Executed with a motion that looks identical to a heavy-spin serve, but with a contact point that imparts little to no actual spin on the ball. Because the visual cues (racket speed, brushing motion, follow-through) look the same as a spin-heavy serve, opponents often over-adjust their racket angle and mishit the return, either popping it up or driving it into the net.

Use when: Mixed in occasionally against an opponent who's reading your spin serves well, to punish overcompensation.

How to Actually Build These Into Your Game

Trying to learn all 10 serves at once is a common mistake, most players get more value from mastering two or three serves deeply, with genuinely disguised spin variations, than from having a shallow version of all ten. A good starting combination is the pendulum serve, the short backspin serve, and one deceptive variation like the no-spin ghost serve, since together they cover short and long placement, multiple spin types, and a disguise element.

Serve development is also one of the areas where a coach's feedback speeds things up the most, since small differences in contact point, wrist timing, and toss consistency are hard to self-diagnose without a second set of eyes watching closely.
