Why Some Table Tennis Players Look Unorthodox but Are Extremely Effective

Their loop doesn't look textbook, their backswing looks strange, and the ball still lands on the table every time. Here's why unconventional technique can beat beautiful technique.

Pong Home Insights Hub · Technique · 7 min read
PH
Pong Home Insights Hub · Coach-written technique guide

Have you ever watched an older or experienced table tennis player and thought "that technique doesn't look right"? Their loop may not look textbook, their backswing might be unusual, and their follow-through may look strange. Yet somehow, the ball keeps landing on the table, the loop is powerful, the placement is accurate, and the spin is heavy. Meanwhile, another player with beautiful, textbook strokes falls apart the moment the pressure increases. Here's why that happens, and what it actually means to have "good" technique.

Good Technique Isn't Always Good Looking Technique

Coaches teach standardized technique for a reason. A textbook stroke provides a strong foundation for learning proper balance, timing, racket acceleration, and body rotation. But experienced players don't always continue to look textbook. After thousands of hours of playing, players naturally adapt their strokes to their own body, equipment, timing, and playing style, and eventually develop movements that look unusual but work extremely well for them.

The important question isn't "does the stroke look perfect?" It's "does the player consistently produce the ball they want?"

Timing Can Beat Beautiful Technique

One of the biggest differences between experienced and less experienced players is timing. A player doesn't need a huge backswing or a dramatic follow-through to produce a powerful loop. If they make contact at the right moment, with the right racket angle and acceleration, they can generate tremendous spin and speed with a movement that looks small and relaxed. They aren't necessarily using more effort, they're using better timing.

They Have Developed Incredible Feel

After decades of playing, experienced players develop something that's difficult to teach from a textbook: feel. They can sense the difference between a heavy backspin ball and a lighter one, adjust to a ball that kicks slightly differently after the bounce, and change their racket angle almost instinctively. Sometimes their stroke looks strange simply because they're making tiny adjustments that aren't obvious to the eye. A beginner might need a large, technically correct movement to handle a particular ball, while an experienced player makes a small adjustment with the wrist, forearm, or body and produces an even better shot.

They Don't Need to Think About Every Movement

A developing player might consciously run through a checklist: open the racket angle, drop the arm, rotate the waist, accelerate forward, finish the stroke. An experienced player isn't thinking about any of that. They see the ball and react. Years of repetition have turned the movement into something almost automatic, which is why some veteran players look incredibly relaxed while producing a very heavy ball. Their technique isn't necessarily simpler, their decision making has become simpler.

Consistency Comes From Repeatability

This is perhaps the most important lesson. A technically beautiful stroke doesn't automatically produce a consistent ball. Imagine two players. Player A has a beautiful textbook loop, their body rotation looks perfect, and their follow-through is excellent, but their timing changes slightly from ball to ball. Player B has an unusual looking loop, a short backswing, and a follow-through that looks strange, but they contact the ball at almost exactly the same point every time.

Player B may end up far more consistent, because ultimately, table tennis isn't judged by how beautiful the stroke looks. The ball only responds to what happens at contact.

Tip: When you're studying a player's technique, watch where and when the racket meets the ball, not just how the backswing and follow-through look. Contact point is where the result actually gets decided.

Experience Changes What "Good Technique" Means

When you're learning, copying good technique is extremely important. But as you become more experienced, technique becomes increasingly personal. Your height, flexibility, strength, reaction time, equipment, playing distance, and preferred tactics all influence how you move. Two excellent players can use completely different looking strokes and still achieve the same objective, which is why trying to force every advanced player into exactly the same technical model can sometimes be counterproductive. The goal isn't to make everyone look the same, it's to help players develop a movement that is efficient, repeatable, and effective.

Don't Confuse Unusual With Inefficient

Some unusual techniques really are inefficient and can limit a player's development, that distinction matters. But an unconventional stroke that's been refined over decades can be incredibly effective. You may look at an older player's loop and think "I would never teach that stroke," and then realize they've just hit twenty balls in a row, all with heavy spin, deep placement, and almost no unforced errors. At that point, the stroke deserves some respect. Maybe it doesn't look textbook, but it works.

Keep in mind: if you're rebuilding your own technique or trying to fix a habit that's holding you back, the goal is a stroke you can repeat under pressure, not one that matches a demonstration video frame for frame. Working with a coach helps separate genuine flaws from personal adaptations worth keeping.

Common Questions About Unorthodox Table Tennis Technique

Is unorthodox technique bad in table tennis?

Not by itself. Some unusual strokes really are inefficient and limit development, but an unconventional technique refined over years of play can be extremely effective. What matters is whether the player can produce the ball they want, consistently, not whether the stroke matches a coaching video.

Should beginners copy the unusual technique of experienced players?

No. Standardized technique gives beginners a strong foundation in balance, timing, and racket acceleration. Unusual strokes that work for veteran players are usually the result of years of adaptation on top of solid fundamentals, not a shortcut to skip those fundamentals.

What matters more than how a table tennis stroke looks?

Timing, feel, and repeatability matter more than appearance. A player who contacts the ball at almost exactly the same point every time will often be more consistent than a player with textbook form whose timing shifts from ball to ball.

The Real Lesson

Beautiful technique is valuable, but it's only one part of becoming a good table tennis player. Timing, ball reading, touch, adaptability, and repetition matter just as much. Sometimes the player with the prettiest stroke isn't the player with the best stroke, and sometimes the player whose technique looks strange is the one who's spent decades figuring out exactly what works. So the next time you see an older player with an unconventional loop, don't judge the stroke too quickly. Watch the ball. If it's heavy, accurate, powerful, and consistently landing on the table, there may be a lot more going on than meets the eye.
