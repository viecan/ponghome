How to Find Your Opponent's Weakness in a Table Tennis Match

Most players guess at what to attack. A short scouting process in the warm up and the first few points tells you exactly where to put the ball instead.

Pong Home Team · Technique · 6 min read
PH
Pong Home Team · Coach-written tactical guide

Strong players lose to weaker opponents all the time for one simple reason: they never actually figure out what to attack. Finding a weakness isn't about luck or years of experience against a specific style. It's a short, repeatable process you can run in the warm up and the first few points of any match. Here's how to do it on purpose instead of by accident.

Start Scouting in the Warm Up

The warm up is not just for loosening your arm. It's free information, and most players waste it by hitting on autopilot. While you rally, pay attention to which side your opponent sets up on before the ball even arrives, how far they have to reach for wide balls, and whether their backhand looks tentative compared to their forehand. A player who steps around the backhand corner to use forehand even on balls hit straight at their body is already showing you where they're most comfortable, and where they aren't.

  • Watch the ready position between shots, since a slow return to center often points to a footwork weakness later in the match.
  • Notice which shots they choose to hit softer during warm up, even when a harder shot was available.
  • Check their serve receive stance, since players often stand slightly favoring their stronger side before the first point is even played.

Test Both Wings Early, Not Just the Backhand

A common mistake is assuming the backhand is automatically the weaker side and building an entire game plan around that guess. In the first few points, send a similar quality ball, same pace, same depth, to both the forehand and backhand corners, and compare how your opponent handles each one. Look at contact point, balance, and shot quality rather than just whether the ball comes back. A weakness usually shows up as a rushed or defensive shot under pressure, not necessarily a missed ball.

Tip: Keep the test balls close in quality to each other. If one test shot is noticeably harder than the other, you're comparing pressure, not comparing sides.

Look for Footwork Gaps, Not Just Shot Weaknesses

Some players have technically sound strokes on both wings but still have a clear weakness: they struggle to move. Pulling a ball wide to one corner and then immediately following with a ball to the opposite corner reveals a lot about recovery speed. If your opponent consistently arrives late or off balance on the second ball, that gap in footwork is just as exploitable as a weak backhand, and it's often easier to target since it doesn't depend on shot placement alone.

Pay Attention to What They Avoid

Players tend to steer away from patterns that expose their own weakness, often without realizing it. If your opponent consistently runs around their backhand to loop with forehand, consistently pushes rather than attacks on one side, or rushes the point whenever a rally gets long, each of those habits is a clue. The shot a player avoids under pressure usually tells you more than the shot they choose to show off.

Serve Into the Gap You Found

Once you've identified a weaker side or a footwork gap, your serve becomes the fastest way to put your opponent there before the rally even starts. Placement matters more than spin here: a well placed short or long serve to the identified weak zone forces your opponent to play from a disadvantage on the very first shot. For more on building serve variety and placement into your game, see our guide on Serve & Return Mechanics.

Watch out: Don't overcommit to one target so early that your pattern becomes obvious. Mix in enough variety that your opponent can't simply cover the weak side and neutralize what you found.

Reassess as the Match Goes On

A weakness you find in the first game isn't guaranteed to hold for the whole match. Good opponents adjust, footwork improves once nerves settle, and a shot that looked shaky early can tighten up under repetition. Keep checking your read every few points rather than locking in on one plan and running it blindly to the end.

Putting It All Together

Finding a weakness is less about reading minds and more about paying close attention early and testing deliberately instead of guessing. Watch the warm up, test both wings with equal pressure, check footwork and recovery, and notice what your opponent avoids under stress. Once you've found the gap, use your serve to send them there on your terms. If your own game plan tends to fall apart under pressure rather than your opponent's, our guide on Tips, Techniques & the Mental Game covers how to stay sharp late in close matches.
