Table Tennis Rules & Scoring, Explained

Five of the most common questions about how table tennis is actually played — from what to call the sport, to how points are won, to what happens when a serve clips the net.

Pong Home Team · Rules & Scoring · 6 min read
PH
Pong Home Team · Coach-written rules guide

Table tennis has a reputation for being simple to pick up and hit around, but the actual rulebook has some specifics that trip up even players who've been playing for years — especially around serving. Here are five of the questions we hear most often, answered clearly and with a bit of the "why" behind each rule.

Terminology

Is "Ping Pong" the same thing as Table Tennis?

Short answer: Yes — same sport, different name history.

"Ping-Pong" wasn't originally a generic nickname — it was a registered trademark. In the early 1900s, the English company J. Jaques & Son sold table tennis sets under the brand name "Ping Pong," and the name (an imitation of the sound the celluloid ball made against the paddle) stuck in popular use even after the trademark passed to other companies over the following decades.

When the sport organized internationally, it needed a name that wasn't tied to any one company's trademark. The International Table Tennis Federation was founded in 1926, and "Table Tennis" became — and remains — the sport's official name in competitive and organized play. In everyday conversation, most people still use "ping pong" and "table tennis" interchangeably, but if you're talking to a club, a coach, or a tournament organizer, "table tennis" is the term you'll hear.

Scoring

How many points are in a game?

Short answer: 11 points, win by 2.

A game of table tennis is played to 11 points, and a player has to win by at least a 2-point margin to close it out. If the score reaches 10–10 (deuce), play simply continues — there's no cap — until one player pulls 2 points ahead, whether that ends up being 12–10, 15–13, or beyond.

This wasn't always the format. Before 2001, games were played to 21 points. The change to an 11-point format was made to make matches faster-paced and more spectator-friendly, particularly for TV broadcasts — shorter games meant momentum swings happened more often and matches were easier to follow from the outside. Most competitive matches are played as the best of 5 or best of 7 games.

Serving

What are the rules for serving?

Short answer: Open palm, vertical toss of at least 16 cm, no spin on the toss, struck from behind the end line.

A legal serve has a few specific requirements that are easy to overlook if you've only ever played casually:

  • The ball must start on an open, flat palm — it can't be hidden in the fingers or the hand cupped around it.
  • It must be tossed vertically upward at least 16 centimeters (about 6.3 inches) before being struck.
  • No spin can be applied during the toss itself — the ball needs to go straight up without being brushed, flicked, or spun on the way up.
  • The strike must happen from behind the end line of the table, and above the level of the playing surface, so the server can't lean forward over the table to make contact.

These rules exist mainly to stop players from disguising or "hiding" the contact point of the serve, which would make it nearly impossible for an opponent to read spin or timing. In casual play, these rules are rarely enforced strictly, but in officiated matches an umpire will call a fault for a low toss, a hidden serve, or spin added before contact.

Serving

Does a serve have to go cross-court?

Short answer: Only in doubles.

This is one of the most commonly confused rules, because it depends entirely on whether you're playing singles or doubles.

In doubles, the serve must land in the server's own right-hand service box first, then cross diagonally over the net and land in the receiver's right-hand box. This diagonal, cross-court requirement is what keeps doubles rotation fair and predictable between partners.

In singles, there's no such restriction — a legal serve can land anywhere on the opponent's half of the table, as long as it clears the net and bounces once on your own side first, then once on the opponent's side. This is why singles serves can be placed short, deep, wide to either corner, or straight down the middle, all equally legally.

Serving

What happens when the ball hits the net on a serve?

Short answer: If it still lands legally, it's a "let" — no penalty, just re-serve.

If the ball clips the top of the net during a serve but still clears it and lands within the legal area on the opponent's side, it's called a let. A let serve isn't a fault — it doesn't count against either player, and there's no change to the score. The server simply serves again.

The distinction that matters is what happens after the net contact. If the ball touches the net and then fails to clear it, or lands outside the legal service area (for example, in the wrong box during doubles, or off the table in singles), that's a fault, and the point goes to the receiver — the net clip on its own isn't automatically a let unless the serve is otherwise legal. This rule exists because a net-clipped serve can behave unpredictably through no fault of the server's technique, so the "let and replay" system keeps the point fair for both sides.

Why These Rules Matter Beyond the Rulebook

Knowing these rules isn't just useful for avoiding disputes during a casual game — serve rules in particular directly shape strategy at every level of play. Because singles serves can land anywhere on the table, singles players spend a lot of practice time varying serve placement and spin to keep opponents guessing. In doubles, the cross-court requirement changes footwork and positioning entirely, since both the serve and the return are constrained to a diagonal pattern between partners.

If you're new to competitive or club play, it's worth practicing a few legal tosses and serve motions specifically — many players who've only played casually are surprised the first time an umpire calls a fault on a serve toss that felt completely normal to them.
